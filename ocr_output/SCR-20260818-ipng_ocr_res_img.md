Approximately 50 years ago, primatologist Jane Goodall reported
a similar fission event among the Kasekela chimpanzee community
in Gombe, Tanzania (3). Several adult males, adult females, and
young individuals splintered into a new group. Many of them were
then killed by former groupmates. Sandel et al. studied the social
dynamics of the second group split observed in wild chimpanzees,
which took place in the Ngogo community in Uganda. This group,
which has been followed systematically for nearly 30 years, grew to
become the largest habituated community of chimpanzees (a popu-
lation that tolerates humans) at more than 200 individuals. Then,
it began to split into two spatially and socially segregated parts.
Sandel et al. combined classic observational and modern analyti-
cal techniques to describe and quantify how this fission unfolded.
They found that this change was not abrupt but that there was a
period of partitioning until all association between the two sides
ceased. Before then, network clusters could be detected by analysis
of social associations, but many chimpanzees switched clusters year
by year, and all clearly belonged to a single group. As connecting
ties waned and some individuals that bridged these clusters were
killed by disease, a division into two distinct groups became com-
plete and irreversible. Bonds between pairs of individuals did not
fade randomly but split along the new line that divided the original
group. The relationship between former groupmates turned hostile
within the first years of the split, starting with individuals from
both groups patrolling their new borders and continuing into lethal
violence that targeted adult males and eventually infants.
Most primates live in groups (4), and some species, such as ca-
puchin monkeys (5) and macaques (6), have been observed to split
several times. Across decades of intensive long-term observation,
however, only two group splits have been documented in wild
chimpanzees. The fission among the Ngogo population is the first
such event observed by humans since Gombe and the only case in
which there was no history of food provisioning. The findings of San-
del et al. recontextualize past observations by raising questions about
why other chimpanzee groups split so rarely and the strategies used
to maintain group stability. There are important differences between
chimpanzees and other species, from social systems to ecology, and
between the Ngogo community and other chimpanzee populations,
from degree of human disturbance to predation pressure, which influ-
ence group size. Therefore, it is premature to draw firm conclusions
about the effects of any one factor on sustaining group cohesion or
causing rupture among the Ngogo chimpanzee community or across
species. Nevertheless, Sandel et al's data and analysis provide crucial
information for reconstructing phylogenetic histories and modeling
the socioecological processes that underlie these events.
Bonobos, which are equally closely related to humans, form stable
and distinct groups, just as chimpanzees do (7). Unlike chimpanzees,
however, they do not engage in violent intergroup conflict but form
tolerant multiday associations and cooperate between groups (8). The
first habituated community of wild bonobos, which was established
by primatologist Takayoshi Kano at Wamba in the Democratic Re-
public of the Congo, also split permanently nearly 50 years ago (9).
