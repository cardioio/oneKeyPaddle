Cohesion in the presplit era (1998 to 2014)
Chimpanzees at Ngogo were part of a single, large group during the
first two decades of continuous research that began in 1995 (Fig. 2A).
Like chimpanzees elsewhere, they displayed high fission-fusion
dynamics, forming temporary associations or "parties" that change
throughout the day as individuals move over a common territory
(20, 29). Females typically dispersed at adolescence, whereas males
remained in their group for their entire life. Adult males at Ngogo
formed a linear dominance hierarchy, associated in mixed-sex parties,
hunted together, and cooperated in territorial patrols (30, 31).
Social network analyses confirmed that the Ngogo chimpanzees
were initially one group. To construct networks, we conducted focal
follows of males for 2 to 3 months annually from 1998 to 2024, includ-
ing all adult males and some younger males that regularly traveled
with adult males (n = 77, mean age = 28.7 ± 9.2 years). During focal
follows, we recorded party association, proximity, and grooming.
Females and additional younger males entered the networks through
their interactions with focal subjects. We fused grooming, proximity,
and party networks of males and females (n = 219 individuals) into a
single weighted network each year using an optimization procedure
that maximized structural consistency across consecutive years (32).
We also created proximity networks for male focal subjects because
these individuals were well sampled and the main participants in territo-
rial behavior and lethal aggression (33).
Despite belonging to a single group, the Ngogo chimpanzees exhib-
ited social substructure. To identify dense subnetworks, or "clusters,"
we applied a robust social network metric, the Leiden algorithm (34).
We identified two to four clusters per year from 1998 to 2014 (Fig. 2B).

had been identified during shorter sampling periods (35-37). Cluster
