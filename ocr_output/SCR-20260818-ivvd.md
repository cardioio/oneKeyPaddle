undergo permanent fissions (17), which reveal how group membership

however, permanent fissions appear to reduce feeding competition or
internal social pressures (18, 19) and are not associated with lethal
between-group aggression, which makes it difficult to reconstruct how
affiliative relationships deteriorate into hostility.
Chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes), one of our two closest living rela-
tives, help bridge this gap. Male chimpanzees remain in their natal
groups for life and cooperate in territorial defense and lethal raids
(20-22). Most lethal aggression against mature male chimpanzees
occurs between members of different groups and can be explained by
hostility toward outsiders, but it also occurs among familiar individu-
als (23, 24). Notably, a chimpanzee group at Gombe, Tanzania, was
suspected of fissioning in the 1970s, with males of one postfission group
killing one adult female and all six males in the other group over 4 years
(20, 25). Provisioning and limited observations, however, obscured key
details (26, 27), and for many decades, the case was an anomaly.
Genetic evidence suggests that permanent fissions in chimpanzees are
exceedingly rare, occurring at median intervals of 500 years (28).
In this work, we report a clear and extensively documented perma-
nent fission of a chimpanzee group and the lethal aggression that
followed. We analyzed 24 years of social networks, 10 years of GPS
based ranging, and 30 years of demographic data from the Ngogo
chimpanzees in Kibale National Park, Uganda. We identified three key
stages: (i) an abrupt shift from cohesion to polarization, defined as
the consolidation of relationships into two cohesive clusters with re-
duced cross-cluster interaction; (ii) 2 years of increasing avoidance
that led to the formation of two groups; and (iii) lethal aggression
between former group members (Fig. 1). These findings provide evi-
dence that shifting relationships, independent of cultural markers, can
fracture a community and catalyze collective violence.
